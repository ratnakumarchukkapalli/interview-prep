Section 20: The FDE & AI Engineer Role Decoded

What the job actually is, how the interview is structured, where your eight years of platform engineering is an advantage rather than a detour, and exactly what to fix in the next eight weeks.

Section 20 — Week 1, Day 1 ~30 min read Read this before anything else
Read this guide once, carefully, before you touch any technical material. The most common way a strong engineer fails an FDE loop is not a knowledge gap — it is preparing for the wrong interview. The FDE loop weights problem decomposition and customer communication roughly as heavily as it weights code, and almost nobody prepares for those.
1

What a Forward Deployed Engineer Actually Is

Palantir invented the role in 2005. Their first customers — intelligence agencies — had problems that traditional consultants could not solve, because the consultants could not write code, and problems that traditional product engineers could not solve, because the product engineers were not allowed near the data. So Palantir put engineers inside the customer and gave them full accountability for the outcome.

In 2026 the model has been copied wholesale by the AI labs. OpenAI and Anthropic both run FDEs as post-sale embedded engineers who own the entire path from “customer has API access” to “system is in production inside the customer's infrastructure, working, and being used.” At Anthropic the function sits under the Applied AI team.

The one-sentence definition: a Forward Deployed Engineer is a software engineer who is embedded in a customer's environment, writes production code against that customer's real data, and is personally accountable for whether the AI system delivers a business outcome — not for whether it was delivered on spec.

The three jobs collapsed into one

Software Engineer
You write and ship production code. In someone else's codebase, in a language you may not have picked, with tests you did not write.
Solutions Architect
You scope the problem, choose the architecture, and decide what not to build. Nobody hands you a design doc.
Customer Success
You sit with the actual users, discover that the stated problem is not the real problem, and manage the politics of saying so.

Two things in that loop are worth staring at. First, the loop starts and ends with the customer, not with a ticket. Second, step G — deploying inside the customer's infrastructure, past their security team, their network, their compliance requirements — is where most AI projects actually die. That step is your existing job description.

The uncomfortable part of the role. FDEs travel, sit in rooms with frustrated non-engineers, do unglamorous data cleanup, and get judged on outcomes they only partly control. If you interview for this role and describe yourself as someone who likes deep focused work on clean systems, you will not get it — and you probably shouldn't. Be honest with yourself about this before week 8.

Strategic read for your situation. You are chasing two roles at once, and they share about 70% of a technical core: LLM fundamentals, RAG, agents, evaluation, and production deployment. This is the single most common mis-preparation: candidates spend three weeks on backpropagation derivations and then cannot explain why their chunking strategy loses cross-reference context. Nobody will ask you to derive a gradient. Everyone will ask you why the retrieval returned the wrong passage.

Round 1 — Behavioral / Fit

Tests ownership and communication. The signature questions are “Why an FDE?”, “Tell me about a demanding stakeholder”, and “Describe a complex 0-to-1 project you owned.” Use STAR, and weight your stories toward shipping code and solving problems without being told to.

Your specific risk here. You have excellent stories — the Ops Assistant, PostgreSQL incidents, the Outposts work — but they are currently told as infrastructure stories. “I built a multi-agent system with 30 tools and CIAM M2M auth” is an architecture statement. An FDE panel wants: “Engineers were spending 40 minutes triaging a failing service by hand. I sat with three of them, watched what they actually typed, and built an assistant that does the first 80% of that triage. It's now used by N engineers and the median triage is under X minutes.” Same project. Completely different signal. Guide 28 rebuilds all your stories this way.

Round 2 — Technical Deep Dive

Two halves. The coding half is deliberately not LeetCode-flavoured: expect practical tasks like parsing a messy data file and building a query API over it. That is a real FDE Tuesday. The design half is data-heavy: real-time analytics pipelines, RAG architecture, extraction pipelines.

What separates a 3 from a 5 here is production instinct. Anyone can sketch documents → embeddings → vector DB → LLM. The follow-up is always some version of “now the customer has 40 million documents, a 2-second p95 SLO, and a compliance rule that every answer must cite a verifiable source.” That is the actual interview.

Round 3 — The Decomposition / Case Study Round

This is the Palantir-invented round, the highest-weighted, and the one you have never practised. You get a massive ambiguous problem and 60 minutes. There is no correct answer. They are watching how you think when nothing is defined.

The Thomson Reuters HackerRank Gate

Before any human speaks to you, TR's internal process puts an automated assessment in front of you. For AI/ML-tagged roles it is a blend, and the mix shifts by role:

ComponentWhat appearsTime pressure
Python DS&A (1–2 problems) LeetCode easy/medium: string manipulation and regex, sliding window, matrix traversal, graph traversal, dynamic programming, greedy, intervals High
Applied ML case (1) Implement a basic recommender, perform model evaluation, or select features from noisy data Moderate
SQL Joins, GROUP BY, and window functions — ROW_NUMBER, RANK, LAG/LEAD, running totals Moderate
Domain math (sometimes) TR is a financial and legal data company: CAGR, NPV, tax impact calculations Low
This is the gate that actually eliminates senior infrastructure engineers, and it does so for a boring reason: DS&A speed is a perishable skill, and platform work does not exercise it. You write excellent YAML, Terraform, and FastAPI handlers. None of that keeps you fast at “longest substring without repeating characters” under a 20-minute clock.

It also cannot be crammed. This is why the plan puts a 20-minute coding drill at the start of every single day for eight weeks — 40 sessions, roughly 80 problems. That is the entire intervention. Do not move it to the end of the session, because that is where it will get skipped.

The same applies to SQL window functions. You know PostgreSQL internals better than most application developers — WAL, replication slots, PITR, vacuum. But LAG(...) OVER (PARTITION BY ... ORDER BY ...) is a different muscle, and it is the one they test. Weeks 2 and 3 each carry 15 queries.

The Skill Matrix — What They Actually Screen For

FDE hiring looks for a T-shape, or more precisely an I-shape with a wide bar: real depth in one technical vertical, plus a broad set of execution skills that most deep engineers lack.

Anthropic's FDE specification asks explicitly for “production experience with LLMs including advanced prompt engineering, agent development, evaluation frameworks, and deployment at scale.” Read that list again: you have agent development and deployment at scale. You need prompt engineering as a discipline and evaluation frameworks. That is a six-week problem, not a two-year one.

The horizontal bar — execution skills

Customer empathy
Can you explain a vector index to a tax partner without condescension, and hear what they actually need?
Radical ownership
The problem is yours until the outcome exists. “That's the platform team's job” is a failing answer.
Decomposition under chaos
Turning “make our lawyers faster” into four buildable components in an hour.
Product sense
Spotting when a one-off customer hack is actually a product feature — and when it isn't.
Autonomy in unfamiliar code
Making progress on day two in a 400K-line codebase with no docs and no one to ask.
Security awareness
Table stakes. Also your home turf — use it.

Background predictors they look for

  1. Early-stage startup engineer — the top predictor, because scope ambiguity was the default condition
  2. Hands-on solutions architect who built real PoCs, not slideware
  3. Data/ML engineer with production deployment experience
  4. Full-stack developer with product intuition

You match none of these four cleanly — and that is fine, because what these four have in common is breadth plus accountability, and platform engineering at a regulated financial-data company is a legitimate fifth path to the same thing. You have owned production incidents where the buck stopped with you. That is the underlying signal. Say so directly rather than hoping they infer it.

DevOps as the Moat, Not the Handicap

The instinct when moving from platform engineering into AI roles is to feel behind — to treat eight years of Kubernetes as a detour from the ML career you should have had. That instinct is wrong, and if it leaks into your interview it will cost you the offer.

Here is the actual market reality. There are a great many engineers who can build a working RAG demo in a notebook in an afternoon. Of those, the number who can do the following is small:

  • Run it multi-tenant on Kubernetes with mTLS and hard per-tenant isolation
  • Put an evaluation gate in the deploy pipeline so a prompt change cannot ship a quality regression
  • Trace a p99 latency spike from the ingress through to a slow ANN query and prove where the time went
  • Reason about the cost curve when the customer's corpus grows 50× and the bill grows 200×
  • Perform a point-in-time recovery when someone truncates the embeddings table on a Friday
  • Get the whole thing through a customer's security review
That list is not adjacent to the FDE job description. It is the FDE job description. The gap between an AI demo and an AI system in production is overwhelmingly infrastructure, security, and evaluation. That is the gap you have spent eight years living in.

Your positioning line

“You're a DevOps engineer. Why should we hire you for an AI role?”
“I've spent eight years making distributed systems work in production under real constraints — regulated environments, on-prem Outposts deployments, security reviews, 3am incidents where the buck stopped with me. And for the last stretch I've been building an agentic assistant that engineers use to operate Kubernetes: multi-step tool calling, real write access to production, real consequences for getting it wrong.

What I've learned from that is that the hard part of an AI system in production isn't the model. It's the retrieval quality nobody's measuring, the token bill nobody's modelled, the tenant isolation nobody's tested, and the fact that there's no gate stopping a prompt change from shipping a regression. Those are infrastructure and evaluation problems, and they're the ones I'm good at.

I'm not going to tell you I'm an ML researcher. I'm telling you I'm the person who gets the thing actually working inside the customer's environment — which as I understand it is what this role is.”
Two failure modes to avoid.
Apologising: “I don't have a formal ML background, but…” — never open here. FDE is not an ML research role and the apology invents a bar that doesn't exist.
Deflecting: when asked an AI question, retreating to Kubernetes because it's comfortable. If they ask how you'd fix poor retrieval recall, do not answer with observability architecture. Answer the retrieval question, then add the infrastructure dimension as a bonus. The `/fde-interview` skill is instructed to catch you doing this.

The Thomson Reuters Context

You are interviewing internally, which is an advantage you should exploit rather than a formality. Internal candidates who demonstrate business awareness materially outperform external candidates with better raw AI credentials, because the panel already knows the hard part is understanding the domain.

TR's AI surface area sits across legal, tax, and news. The products to be able to speak about: CoCounsel (the legal AI assistant), Westlaw (legal research), Practical Law (know-how and templates), ONESOURCE and Checkpoint (tax and accounting), and the Reuters news wire. Guide 38 covers this properly in week 8.

The constraint that shapes every technical answer

In legal and tax AI, a confident wrong answer is worse than no answer. A hallucinated case citation can get a lawyer sanctioned. A wrong tax treatment can trigger a filing error. This is not an abstract safety concern — it is the product requirement.

Practically, this means: every claim must be traceable to a retrieved source, retrieval quality matters more than generation fluency, abstention (“I couldn't find this”) is a feature not a failure, and evaluation must measure groundedness, not just helpfulness.

Carry this into every technical answer you give. When you're asked to design a RAG system, the fact that you volunteer “and because this is legal content, I'd architecturally guarantee that every cited passage actually contains the claim — I'd verify the citation post-generation rather than trusting the model to quote correctly” is the difference between a competent answer and an answer that sounds like it came from someone who works here.

The daily two hours

0:00 - 0:20   Coding drill      2 problems, timed. Non-negotiable. Every day.
0:20 - 1:20   Topic block       Read the week's guide section
1:20 - 1:50   Hands-on          Build in code/ — type it, don't read it
1:50 - 2:00   Active recall     3 interview questions, out loud, no notes

Four rules that make this work:

  1. Never skip the coding drill. It is first in the session precisely because anything at the end of a session gets skipped. 40 sessions × 2 problems clears the gate.
  2. Type all the code by hand. Reading a RAG pipeline teaches you nothing about debugging one, and the interview is entirely about debugging one.
  3. Active recall beats re-reading. If you cannot say it out loud without notes, you do not know it. Re-reading feels like progress and isn't.
  4. Every guide ends in interview questions. Write your answers first, then compare. The gap between what you thought you knew and what you could produce is the actual study signal.

Self-Test Questions

Answer these out loud, from memory, before moving to Guide 21. If any answer takes more than 60 seconds to start, re-read the relevant section.

1. In one sentence, what is a Forward Deployed Engineer accountable for?
A business outcome inside a customer's environment — not a delivered spec, not a shipped feature. If the customer's metric didn't move, the FDE didn't succeed, regardless of what was built.
2. Which of the three rounds carries the most weight, and what is it actually measuring?
The decomposition case study. It measures how you think when nothing is defined: whether you clarify before solving, cut scope explicitly, decompose into components with independent failure modes, propose a value-delivering MVP that de-risks the biggest unknown, and define measurable success.
3. Why can't the HackerRank gate be crammed in week 8?
Because it tests speed, and speed is built by repetition spaced over time, not by volume in a burst. It is also a hard pass/fail before any human evaluates you — so it is the highest-consequence, lowest-glamour item in the plan.
4. Name three things you can do that a typical strong AI-notebook engineer cannot.
Any three from: multi-tenant deployment with real isolation; an eval gate wired into the deploy pipeline; end-to-end latency attribution through the stack; cost modelling as the corpus scales; PITR when the vector store is damaged; passing a customer security review.
5. What is the constraint that shapes every technical answer you give at Thomson Reuters?
A confident wrong answer is worse than no answer. Citations must be verifiable, retrieval quality dominates generation fluency, abstention is a feature, and evaluation measures groundedness rather than helpfulness.
6. What are your two failure modes in an AI interview, given your background?
Apologising for not having an ML background (invents a bar that doesn't exist for this role), and deflecting AI questions into infrastructure answers because infrastructure is comfortable. Answer the question asked, then add the infrastructure dimension as a bonus.
7. Rewrite this as an FDE would: “I built a multi-agent Kubernetes assistant with 30 tools and CIAM M2M authentication.”
Lead with the human problem and the measured change: who was doing what by hand, how long it took, what you observed by sitting with them, what the assistant now does, how many people use it, and what the before/after number is. The architecture comes second, and only if asked. If you cannot produce the before/after number, that is your homework this week.
